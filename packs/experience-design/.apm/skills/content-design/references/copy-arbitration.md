# Copy arbitration

Named copy goals will pull a single copy choice in opposite directions. "Earned authority" wants the claim before the proof; "Warm directness" wants the reader's feeling before the brand's position. Both can be real goals at the same time. Arbitration is how you decide *before* the conflict reaches a copy choice, so the team resolves it once rather than re-litigating it on every draft.

## The scope parameter

Two modes load this reference, and they arbitrate for different scopes. The method is identical; only the scope changes. Read **the scope** below as:

| Mode | The scope | What a conflict is resolved *for* |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Per-surface acquisition copy goals | one surface | this surface's reader, at this point in their journey |
| Brand-level register | the brand across surfaces | this brand's register, wherever it is read |

Everything that follows is written once against **the scope**. Where a rule genuinely differs between the two, the difference is named inline rather than split into a second file.

## The rule: rank, then a tie has an answer

Two goals of equal weight cannot break a tie — whoever is arguing loudest wins, and the copy drifts. **Rank the goals up front** and name a **dominant goal**: the one that wins when goals genuinely conflict on a real copy choice within the scope. Ranking is the whole mechanism. Everything else records the consequences.

## Common conflict types

Each conflict is resolved against the scope. The per-surface mode decides it for the surface in hand; the brand-level mode decides it as a standing default the surfaces inherit.

**Urgency vs. warmth.** A time-sensitive CTA ("Start today — pricing changes next week") conflicts with a warm, unhurried register ("We will be here when you are ready"). Per surface: a bottom-of-funnel acquisition surface typically favours urgency, while a welcome surface read at varied paces typically favours warmth. Brand-level: a high-urgency conversion posture favours urgency, a warm and unhurried register favours warmth. The conflict is not always present, but when it is, it should be pre-resolved.

**Brevity vs. completeness.** A tweet-test goal ("Say the one thing in eight words") conflicts with a completeness goal ("Give the skeptical reader every objection handled"). Per surface: above-fold copy for a cold audience typically favours brevity, a pricing comparison table for an evaluating buyer typically favours completeness. Brand-level: the same split follows the primary audience rather than the page. Brevity and completeness are both legitimate; which wins is a choice, not a quality judgment.

**Authority vs. approachability.** A "guide-not-guru" goal wants a peer register; an "earned authority" goal wants expertise that precedes the claim. These conflict when the audience is mixed across seniority: senior practitioners may read authority as confidence, while junior users may read it as gatekeeping. Name the primary reader's position on this axis before trying to serve the secondary reader.

**Specificity vs. universality.** A specific claim ("Designed for teams of five to fifty") excludes readers outside that frame; a universal claim ("For teams of every size") is too vague to land. Specificity converts the reader it fits; universality converts no one precisely. Name which goal wins for the scope and its audience.

## How to rank

1. **Order by what the copy cannot survive losing.** Ask, goal by goal: "If we nailed everything except this one, would the copy still do its job for the scope?" The goal that breaks the copy when missing sits at the top.
2. **Force a strict order.** No ties at the top. If two goals feel equal, find the specific copy choice where they would conflict and decide which you would sacrifice — that decision *is* the ranking.
3. **Sanity-check against the felt word.** The dominant goal should match the user's original gut answer from Stage 1 of the interrogation. If the ranking says one thing and their gut said another, surface the gap — one of them is wrong, and it is worth knowing which.

## Record the trade-off, not just the winner

When you resolve a conflict, write down all three parts so the build does not reopen it:

- **The tension** — which two goals pull apart, and on what kind of copy choice (CTA phrasing, headline length, level of proof before the claim).
- **The call** — which goal wins, stated as the dominant goal applied: "When brevity and completeness conflict on above-fold copy, brevity wins."
- **The why** — one sentence tying it to the ranking, so a future reader sees the reasoning, not just the verdict.

A recorded trade-off turns a recurring argument into a settled default. The build can still escalate a specific case — but the burden is on the exception to justify itself, not on the direction to re-prove itself.

## Adding a new conflict type

When a copy choice surfaces a conflict not in the list above, record it in the document this mode writes:
1. Name the two goals that pulled in opposite directions on this choice.
2. Record which won and why.
3. Note whether this is a one-off (record under Open Questions) or a recurring conflict type (add it to the arbitration table).

A document that grows its arbitration table over the course of a build is working as intended — the team is externalizing their copy judgments rather than relitigating them.
