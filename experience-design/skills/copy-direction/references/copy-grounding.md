# Grounding copy goals in stable referents

After the interrogation converges on named copy goals, each goal must be grounded in at least one **stable referent** before it can steer a copy decision. A referent is stable when it exists outside the current session and can be pointed to — so a later copy choice traces back to it, not to a fresh opinion.

## The three referent types

### 1 — Persona language

Grounding a copy goal in persona language means grounding it in the words the audience actually uses — their vocabulary, their framing, their idioms — mined from real interactions with this surface's reader type.

**Sources for persona language:**
- Support ticket transcripts — the words customers use when something is wrong; their language under friction is unfiltered.
- Sales call recordings or notes — how prospects describe the problem they are trying to solve.
- Community posts or forums — the phrasing people use when they talk about the problem among themselves.
- Survey verbatims — open-ended responses that capture the reader's own language.

If VoC data is absent, elicit persona language inline: ask "What words does your audience use when they describe this problem to a colleague?" Flag the resulting goals as **"directional — not backed by VoC research"** until real VoC data is available.

### 2 — Copy precedents

A copy precedent is a named example of copy that carries the quality the goal names — a quality anchor that demonstrates the quality is achievable.

**How to use a precedent:**
Name the precedent, name the specific attribute you are taking from it, and name what you are leaving. For example: "[example service]'s pricing page — the clarity of the claim structure (value before features) — not the premium register, which is too cold for our audience." The named attribute is the referent; the whole example is not.

**What precedents are not:**
- Templates. Never say "write copy like [example service]." Extract which qualities ground the goal.
- Licenses. A precedent shows the quality is achievable; it does not mean the copy should sound like the precedent.

### 3 — Persuasion standards

Named frameworks for evaluating whether copy works. Cite the standard by name and apply it functionally.

**Painkiller-first framing.** The reader's problem before the product's features. A copy goal of "Problem-first voice" is grounded when it names painkiller-first framing as the standard it respects.

**Tweet test.** Can the headline stand alone as a conviction statement in 280 characters or fewer? A copy goal of "Self-contained clarity" is grounded when it names the tweet test as its verification standard.

**Five-second evaluator scan.** A skeptical reader scanning above-fold copy for five seconds should be able to answer: is this for me, and what is the claim? A copy goal of "Immediate relevance" is grounded when it names the five-second scan as its verification standard.

## Relationship to the brand register

If a `tone-of-voice` brand-register doc exists for this product, use it as an additional upstream referent in Step 3. Each per-surface copy goal must be consistent with the brand register. Tension between a per-surface goal and the brand register is an open question, not a silent override — surface it before closing.

## Recording the grounding

For each named copy goal in the copy-direction doc, record:

- **Persona language** — the words the audience actually uses that ground this goal, or "directional — not backed by VoC research."
- **Copy precedent** — one or two named examples that carry the quality, and which specific attribute you are taking from each.
- **Persuasion standard** — the named standard the goal respects (painkiller-first, tweet test, five-second scan).

A goal that clears all three is well-grounded. A goal that clears one or two can proceed with the missing referents recorded as open questions. A goal that clears none goes back to Stage 2 of the interrogation.
